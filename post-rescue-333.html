Adopting an adult rescue dog: the 3-3-3 rule

Rescue · 6 minute read

Most dog advice is written for puppies. If you have brought home an adult dog from a shelter, almost none of it applies to your first month.

The 3-3-3 rule is a rough guide to what settling in actually looks like: 3 days, 3 weeks, 3 months. It is a pattern, not a promise, and plenty of dogs move faster or slower.

First 3 days: shutdown

Your dog has just lost everything it knew for the second or third time. What you see in the first days is often not the dog.

Expect some of: sleeping constantly or not at all, refusing food, hiding, pacing, toilet accidents, no interest in toys or affection, or the opposite, following you into every room including the bathroom.

A dog that is unnaturally quiet and well behaved in week 1 is often not calm. It is shut down.

What to do:

  • Do almost nothing. No visitors, no long walks, no dog parks, no pet shop trips.
  • Give it a defined safe space it can retreat to, and never disturb it there.
  • Keep the lead on indoors for the first days if you need to move the dog without grabbing a collar.
  • Feed on a schedule in a quiet spot. Walk away while it eats.
  • Short, boring walks on the same route. Same is comforting.

First 3 weeks: settling, and the real dog appears

Around week 2 or 3 the dog works out it is staying. Appetite returns, so does personality.

This is also when behaviours appear that were invisible in week 1: barking at the window, guarding the sofa, pulling on the lead, anxiety when you leave. People often think they have been misled by the shelter. Usually the dog was simply too overwhelmed to show any of it.

What to do:

  • Start a routine and keep it identical. Same feed times, same walk times, same bedtime.
  • Begin very short alone-time practice now. 30 seconds behind a door, then a minute. Do not wait until you have to go somewhere.
  • Start basic training, 3 minutes at a time. Name, sit, hand touch. It builds a shared language and it is genuinely calming for a dog with nothing to do.
  • Introduce one new thing at a time, not a weekend of them.

First 3 months: trust

By month 3 most dogs have decided this is home. The bond arrives here, not on day 1, and it arrives quietly.

What people report at this point: the dog starts sleeping properly on its side, greets you at the door, offers behaviours without being asked, and relaxes when you leave the room.

What to do:

  • Widen the world now: new routes, cafes, other dogs, car trips.
  • Keep training going. A rescue dog with a job settles better than one with none.
  • Address any remaining behaviour problems properly rather than hoping they fade. They usually do not.

Six things that make the first month easier

  1. Decide the house rules before the dog arrives and get everyone to agree. Changing the sofa rule in week 3 is unfair and confusing.
  2. Do not hug a new dog, and do not let children do it. It is a primate gesture and most dogs tolerate it at best.
  3. Use a harness and a well-fitted collar with a tag from day 1. Newly adopted dogs bolt, and they do not know their way home.
  4. Do not take the food bowl away. If there is any guarding, walk past and drop something better into the bowl instead. Teach that your approach means more food, not less.
  5. Skip the dog park for at least a month. You do not yet know how your dog handles it, and one bad experience takes months to undo.
  6. Book a vet check in the first 2 weeks. Pain from an undiagnosed problem looks exactly like a behaviour problem.

When to get help sooner

Do not wait out growling over food or objects, stiff silent staring, biting, or panic when left alone. Those need a qualified behaviourist in person, and they respond much better early than after 6 months of practice.

Asking for help in month 1 is not failing. It is the cheapest and kindest version of the same conversation you would otherwise have in month 8.
