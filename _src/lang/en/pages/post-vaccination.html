The puppy vaccination schedule in Europe

Health · 6 minute read

Vaccination protocols vary between countries, vets and individual dogs. This is the standard European pattern so you know what to expect and what to ask. Your vet decides the actual schedule, not an article.

The usual timetable

  • 6 to 8 weeks. First core vaccination. Usually given by the breeder before the puppy goes home.
  • 10 to 12 weeks. Second dose.
  • 14 to 16 weeks. Third dose. Many vets consider the course complete only after this one, because maternal antibodies can block earlier doses.
  • 12 weeks or later. Rabies, where required. Mandatory in much of Europe and required for travel.
  • 12 months. First booster.
  • Then every 1 to 3 years depending on the vaccine and national guidance.

What the core vaccines cover

  • Distemper. Often fatal, affects the nervous system.
  • Parvovirus. Severe and frequently fatal in puppies. This is the one that makes the timing matter.
  • Hepatitis, or adenovirus. Liver disease.
  • Leptospirosis. Bacterial, spread through water and rodent urine, and transmissible to people. Common across Europe.
  • Rabies. Legally required in most European countries and for crossing borders.

Kennel cough is usually optional and separate, and is often required by boarding kennels, day care and training classes.

The socialisation problem, and how to solve it

Here is the tension nobody explains. The socialisation window, when a puppy forms its lasting view of what is normal and safe, runs roughly from 3 to 16 weeks. The vaccination course is not complete until 14 to 16 weeks.

Waiting until full protection means missing most of the window. Under-socialised dogs are far more likely to end up fearful or reactive, and behaviour problems are a bigger cause of dogs being given up than infection is.

You do not have to choose. Ways to socialise safely before the course is finished:

  1. Carry the puppy. Traffic, shops, buses, trams, crowds, umbrellas, wheelie bins, hi-vis jackets. All of it counts and none of it requires paws on the pavement.
  2. Visit friends' homes, where the dogs are known to be vaccinated and healthy.
  3. Use your own garden and other private gardens.
  4. Invite people to you. Different ages, hats, beards, sticks, uniforms.
  5. Attend a proper puppy class that requires proof of first vaccination and disinfects the floor.
  6. Sit in the car with the window down and let the world go past.

Avoid public parks, pavements in high dog-traffic areas, and anywhere with unknown dog faeces until your vet clears you.

Quality beats quantity

Socialisation is not a checklist of 100 experiences. One calm, positive encounter with a bus is worth more than 5 frightening ones.

Watch the puppy, not the list. Ears back, tail tucked, trying to move away, refusing food it normally takes: all mean you are too close. Increase distance and let it watch from there.

Practical points

  • Keep the vaccination card safe. Boarding, day care, classes and travel all ask for it.
  • Mild tiredness for 24 hours after a jab is normal. Swelling of the face, vomiting or difficulty breathing is not. Ring the vet.
  • Book the next appointment while you are there. Being 3 weeks late is the most common way a course goes wrong.
  • Travelling in Europe requires a pet passport, a microchip, and rabies vaccination at least 21 days before travel. Some countries add a tapeworm treatment. Check the destination rules early, not the week before.

What to ask your vet

  1. When is my puppy fully protected, and what can we do before then?
  2. Is leptospirosis 2-strain or 4-strain here, and which do you recommend?
  3. Do we need rabies for where I live, and when for travel?
  4. When is the first booster, and what happens after that?
  5. Which parasites matter in this region, and on what schedule?
